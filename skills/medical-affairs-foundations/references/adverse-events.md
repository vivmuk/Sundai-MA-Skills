## 1. What counts as an adverse event

Per ICH E2A, an adverse event is **any untoward medical occurrence in a patient
or clinical investigation subject administered a pharmaceutical product, which
does not necessarily have a causal relationship with this treatment.**

The phrase that does the work is *does not necessarily have a causal
relationship*. An AE is any unfavourable and unintended sign, symptom, abnormal
laboratory finding, or disease temporally associated with the product's use.

**Relatedness is not a filter you apply.** Causality assessment is performed by
qualified pharmacovigilance staff with the full case. If you find yourself
thinking "that's obviously just their underlying disease" or "that can't be the
drug", report it anyway. Signal detection depends on the denominator of reported
cases, and pre-filtering by a non-expert systematically destroys it.

An **adverse drug reaction** is the subset where a causal relationship is at
least a reasonable possibility. Distinguishing the two is PV's job, not yours.

**Unexpected** means not consistent with the applicable product information —
the Investigator's Brochure for an unapproved product, or the approved labelling
(SmPC / USPI) for a marketed one. Unexpectedness drives expedited reporting, and
determining it requires the current reference document.

## 2. Seriousness criteria

An AE is **serious** if it results in any of the following (ICH E2A):

1. **Death**
2. **Life-threatening** — the patient was at risk of death *at the time of the
   event*. Not an event that hypothetically might have been fatal if more severe.
3. **Inpatient hospitalisation or prolongation of existing hospitalisation**
4. **Persistent or significant disability or incapacity** — substantial
   disruption of the ability to conduct normal life functions
5. **Congenital anomaly or birth defect**
6. **Other medically important event** — requires medical or surgical
   intervention to prevent one of the outcomes above. Examples conventionally
   included: intensive treatment for allergic bronchospasm, blood dyscrasias or
   convulsions not resulting in hospitalisation, development of drug dependency
   or abuse.

**Serious ≠ severe.** Severity describes intensity (mild / moderate / severe);
seriousness is a regulatory category driven by outcome. A severe headache is
usually non-serious. A mild transient ischaemic attack requiring hospitalisation
is serious. Confusing the two is the single most common classification error in
field-sourced material, and it changes the reporting clock.

## 3. The four elements of a valid case

A valid Individual Case Safety Report requires:

1. An **identifiable patient** — not necessarily named. Age, sex, initials, or
   "a patient of mine" from an identifiable reporter can suffice.
2. An **identifiable reporter** — someone contactable for follow-up.
3. A **suspect medicinal product**
4. An **event or outcome**

**Report cases missing elements anyway.** PV performs follow-up to complete
them. A common and damaging field-staff error is discarding an incomplete report
as "not a real case". It is a real case with missing information, and the
follow-up is a defined process.

Literature and social-media sourced cases have their own rules; route them
through PV rather than deciding yourself.

## 4. Special situations

These require reporting whether or not an adverse event occurred alongside them,
under most company SOPs and EU GVP Module VI:

| Situation | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Pregnancy exposure** | Maternal or paternal, any timing. Report the exposure; follow up for outcome. |
| **Breastfeeding exposure** | Including exposure via breast milk. |
| **Overdose** | Accidental or intentional, with or without symptoms. |
| **Misuse** | Intentional and inappropriate use outside the label. |
| **Abuse** | Persistent or sporadic intentional excessive use with harmful effects. |
| **Medication error** | Including near-misses and intercepted errors. |
| **Occupational exposure** | Healthcare worker, manufacturing, or caregiver exposure. |
| **Off-label use** | Report where an outcome is described; many SOPs require reporting all instances. |
| **Lack of therapeutic effect** | Particularly critical for anti-infectives, vaccines, contraceptives, and life-sustaining products. |
| **Transmission of an infectious agent** | Suspected transmission via the product. |
| **Use in paediatric or elderly populations outside the label** | |
| **Suspected falsified or counterfeit product** | Escalate urgently — this is a public-health issue. |
| **Drug interaction** | Where an unexpected interaction is described. |

## 5. Product quality complaints

A PQC is any suspected deficiency in the identity, quality, durability,
reliability, safety, effectiveness, or performance of a product. Examples:
discoloration, particulate matter, broken or damaged product, packaging or
labelling defects, device malfunction (autoinjector failing to fire,
pen misdosing), suspected contamination, and unexpected taste or odour.

**A PQC and an AE frequently co-occur and must both be reported.** An
autoinjector that fails to deliver a full dose is a PQC; if the patient's
disease flared as a result, that is also an AE. Reporting only one is a common
and consequential miss.

Suspected falsification or counterfeiting is escalated immediately through both
quality and security channels.

## 6. Timelines

The regulatory clocks that matter downstream:

| Report type | Regulatory timeline |
|---|---|
| Serious, unexpected, related (post-approval, expedited) | **15 calendar days** from company awareness (21 CFR 314.80; EU GVP VI) |
| Fatal or life-threatening, unexpected (IND safety report) | **7 calendar days** for initial notification (21 CFR 312.32) |
| Serious, expected / non-serious | Periodic reporting (PADER / PSUR / PBRER) |
| Non-serious, EEA | **90 calendar days** under GVP VI |

**Day zero is the day any company employee or agent became aware** — including
an MSL reading a note, or an agent surfacing it to that MSL. This is why
internal SOPs almost always require forwarding to PV within **one business day**
or **24 hours**: the internal deadline exists to protect the regulatory one.

Follow your own SOP timeline. It will be tighter than the regulatory one, by
design.

## 7. Where these hide in Medical Affairs source material

The cases that get missed are rarely labelled. In field notes and interaction
records, watch for:

- *"He's had a couple of patients stop because of the rash"* — AEs, plural,
  with an outcome (discontinuation).
- *"She's using it in second line and seeing good responses"* — off-label use.
- *"One of his patients got pregnant while on study"* — pregnancy exposure.
- *"They had to admit a patient for the cytokine release"* — serious AE
  (hospitalisation).
- *"The pens have been jamming"* — PQC, possibly multiple.
- *"It just stopped working for a couple of his patients"* — lack of effect.
- *"The nurse gave the loading dose twice"* — medication error.
- *"Doesn't seem to work as well in the older patients"* — potential lack of
  effect in a subpopulation; assess.

In congress and advisory board material: investigator anecdotes, case
discussions, and Q&A frequently contain reportable content. In medical
information requests: the question itself often embeds a case
(*"my patient developed X after Y — what do you know about this?"*).

**Non-identified aggregate published data is not a reportable case.** A safety
table in a published trial is literature, not an ICSR. Individual case reports in
the literature concerning your company's product *are* subject to literature
monitoring obligations — route them to PV, do not assume they are already known.

## 8. What you must not do

- **Do not assess causality.** Not yours to do.
- **Do not decide reportability.** Report and let PV decide.
- **Do not de-duplicate.** Two mentions that look like the same case may not be.
  PV has the tools and the case data to determine this; you do not.
- **Do not paraphrase the verbatim.** Clinical detail determines seriousness.
  Reproduce the original words, redacting only direct patient identifiers.
- **Do not delay to complete your analysis.** Surface the finding immediately,
  then continue.
- **Do not present your scan as a control.** Say plainly that it is an aid, that
  it may miss cases, and that the human's obligations are unchanged.
- **Do not write an ICSR.** Producing a formal case report is a regulated
  activity performed in a validated system by trained staff.

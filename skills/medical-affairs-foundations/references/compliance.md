# The Compliance Framework Around Medical Affairs

Read this when a decision turns on a specific standard rather than on the
general principle in the SKILL.md body.

draft guidance in particular can sit in draft for years and then change
materially on finalisation. Dates below are given so you can check. Where a
decision has real consequence, verify the current version at the source rather
than relying on this summary. Nothing here is legal advice; your compliance

## 1. United States

### The statutory base

- **FDCA § 502(n)** — prescription drug advertising requirements.
- **21 CFR 202.1** — prescription drug advertisements; the fair-balance
  requirement lives here.
- **21 CFR 314.81(b)(3)(i)** — promotional labelling and advertising submitted
  to FDA at time of first use (Form FDA 2253).
- **21 CFR 99** — dissemination of information on unapproved uses (largely
  superseded in practice by the guidance framework below).

Communications that meet the definition of labelling or advertising are subject
to these requirements. Genuine scientific exchange is not intended to be, which
is precisely why the boundary matters so much.

### The guidance that governs Medical Affairs practice

| Guidance | Status | What it addresses |
|---|---|---|
| **Responding to Unsolicited Requests for Off-Label Information About Prescription Drugs and Medical Devices** | Draft, Dec 2011 | The reactive pathway. Distinguishes solicited from unsolicited; public vs. private requests; recommends responses be private, tailored to the requester, delivered by medical/scientific staff, and recorded. |
| **Communications From Firms to Health Care Providers Regarding Scientific Information on Unapproved Uses (SIUU)** | Draft, Oct 2023 | Supersedes the earlier reprint-practices approach. Sets expectations that SIUU communications be truthful, non-misleading, factually supported by scientifically sound evidence, and clearly distinguished from promotional communications, with prescribed disclosures. |
| **Medical Product Communications That Are Consistent With the FDA-Required Labeling (CFL)** | Final, June 2018 | Three-factor test for whether a communication is consistent with labelling. If it is, it is not evaluated as evidence of a new intended use — but must still be truthful and non-misleading and supported by appropriate evidence. |
| **Drug and Device Manufacturer Communications With Payors, Formulary Committees, and Similar Entities** | Final, June 2018 | The FDAMA 114 pathway for health-care economic information to payers, and pre-approval exchange of unapproved-product information with payers. Note the audience limitation — this is not an HCP pathway. |

**FDAMA 114** (FDCA § 502(a)) permits health-care economic information relating
to an approved indication to be provided to payer audiences where it is based on
competent and reliable scientific evidence. It does not open a general channel to
prescribers.

### The reactive pathway in practice

Medical Affairs can discuss unapproved uses, but only through a narrow,
genuinely reactive pathway. Getting this wrong is one of the highest-consequence
errors in the function.

**What is permitted, broadly:**

- Responding to a genuine **unsolicited** request from a healthcare professional
  — one the company did not prompt, encourage, or engineer.
- Responding with truthful, non-misleading, scientifically balanced information
  that is factually supported.
- Routing the response through Medical Information rather than a field
  promotional channel, tailored to the specific requester, and recorded.
- Stating the approval status explicitly and prominently.
- Including safety information and the limitations of the supporting data.

**What is not:**

- Proactively raising unapproved uses, or asking questions engineered to
  generate a "request".
- Treating a question asked at a company-organised promotional event as
  unsolicited.
- Providing a response broader than the question asked.
- Any comparative or superiority framing on an unapproved use.

The approval-status statement belongs in the body, not a footnote:

> *[Product] is not approved for [use] in [jurisdiction]. The following
> information is provided in response to your specific request and describes
> investigational data. Efficacy and safety have not been established for this
> use.*

**Guidance changes.** Where a decision turns on the precise standard, verify the
current version rather than relying on this summary.

### Enforcement context

- **OIG Compliance Program Guidance for Pharmaceutical Manufacturers** (2003,
  with subsequent OIG guidance) — the anti-kickback lens. Advisory boards,
  speaker programmes, consulting arrangements, and research funding are all
  examined for whether they are genuine, fair-market-value, and not disguised
  inducement to prescribe.
- **OIG Special Fraud Alert on speaker programmes** (Nov 2020) — a useful and
  blunt statement of what regulators consider suspect: lavish venues, repeat
  attendance, minimal educational content, attendees with no legitimate need.
- **Corporate Integrity Agreements** — where enforcement has landed, CIAs
  frequently impose specific requirements on the separation of Medical Affairs
  from Commercial, including reporting lines, incentive compensation, and
  restrictions on field-medical involvement in commercial activity.

The recurring theme in enforcement is **pretext**: a medical activity whose real
purpose is promotional. An advisory board with 40 advisors and no genuine
question, an MSL deployed against a sales target, a publication plan built to
support a launch message rather than to communicate science.

## 2. European Union and UK

- **Directive 2001/83/EC, Title VIII / VIIIa** — advertising of medicinal
  products. Article 87(3) requires that advertising encourage rational use and
  not be misleading. Advertising to the general public of prescription-only
  medicines is prohibited (Article 88), a materially stricter position than the
  US.
- **Regulation (EU) 536/2014 (Clinical Trials Regulation)** — includes the
  requirement (Article 37) for a summary of results in language understandable
  to laypersons, submitted to the EU database. This is the driver behind the
  `plain-language-summary` skill.
- **EU GVP Modules** — pharmacovigilance system requirements; Module VI governs
  collection and submission of suspected adverse reaction reports.
- **UK: the ABPI Code of Practice** (administered by the PMCPA) is unusually
  detailed on medical function conduct, and its rulings are published. Because
  the case reports are public, the ABPI rulings are one of the more useful
  practical guides available to anyone anywhere on where the line actually sits.

The EU/UK position differs from the US in ways that matter operationally:
information provision to HCPs is more tightly constrained, the concept of
"non-promotional" is scrutinised more actively, and the distinction between
medical and commercial roles is expected to be structural rather than declared.

## 3. International and industry codes

- **IFPMA Code of Practice** — global baseline for member companies.
- **EFPIA Code of Practice** — Europe; consolidated code covering both HCP and
  patient-organisation interactions, and the disclosure requirements.
- **PhRMA Code on Interactions with Health Care Professionals** — US, voluntary
  but effectively expected; revised with effect from January 2022 to strengthen
  provisions on speaker programmes and meals.
- **MHRA / national codes** and **local affiliate codes** frequently add
  requirements. Assume the local code is stricter than the international one.

Industry codes are voluntary in form and mandatory in practice: adherence is a
condition of trade-association membership, breaches are published, and
regulators cite them.

## 4. Transparency

- **US Physician Payments Sunshine Act** (42 U.S.C. § 1320a-7h), reported via
  CMS **Open Payments**. Since the 2021 expansion, covered recipients include
  physicians, physician assistants, nurse practitioners, clinical nurse
  specialists, certified registered nurse anaesthetists, and certified
  nurse-midwives, plus teaching hospitals.
- **EFPIA Disclosure Code** — annual disclosure of transfers of value to HCPs
  and healthcare organisations in Europe.
- National regimes — France (Loi Bertrand / *Transparence Santé*), and
  equivalents elsewhere, some with criminal penalties.

Reportable transfers include advisory board honoraria, consulting fees, speaker
fees, travel and accommodation, meals, research funding, and grants.

**The practical test for any proposed engagement:** it will be published against
the individual's name. Would it be defensible in that form, to that person's
colleagues and patients?

## 5. Publications and authorship

- **ICMJE Recommendations** — four authorship criteria, *all* of which must be
  met: (1) substantial contribution to conception/design or acquisition,
  analysis, or interpretation; (2) drafting or critical revision for important
  intellectual content; (3) final approval of the version to be published;
  (4) agreement to be accountable for all aspects of the work.
- **GPP 2022** (Good Publication Practice, *Annals of Internal Medicine*, 2022)
  — the consensus standard for company-sponsored medical research publication.
  Covers publication steering committees, author selection, professional medical
  writing support and its disclosure, data access for authors, and the
  obligation to publish results regardless of outcome.
- **CONSORT / PRISMA / STROBE / CARE / SPIRIT** — reporting guidelines by study
  design, catalogued by the EQUATOR Network.

**Ghost authorship** (a substantive contributor unnamed) and **guest authorship**
(a named author who does not meet the criteria) are both serious integrity
breaches. Professional medical writing support is entirely legitimate *and must
be disclosed*, with the funder named.

Non-publication of negative results is a research-integrity failure, not merely
a commercial choice.

## 6. Where the lines actually get crossed

Patterns worth recognising, because they rarely announce themselves:

**The engineered unsolicited request.** A field team member raises a topic in a
way designed to prompt a question, then answers it as though reactive. The
request must be genuinely unprompted.

**The advisory board with a predetermined answer.** If the company already knows
what it wants to hear, it is not seeking advice. Genuine advisory boards have
questions the company cannot answer, a defensible number of advisors,
documented output that influenced a decision, and fair-market-value payment.

**Field medical as a commercial channel.** MSL activity directed by sales
targets, aligned to prescriber deciles, or compensated on product uptake. The
separation must be structural — reporting lines, objectives, incentive
compensation — not declared.

**Publication plans built backwards from a message.** A plan that starts with
the desired narrative and selects the data to support it, rather than starting
from what the evidence supports and what clinical questions remain open.

**Scientific-platform language that is quietly promotional.** A medical
narrative that only ever presents favourable data, that omits the comparator
context, or that uses "differentiated" as a euphemism for "better" without a
head-to-head trial.

**Selective congress reporting.** Summarising a congress in a way that
foregrounds favourable competitor weaknesses and background one's own product's
weaknesses. `congress-intelligence` is written to resist this specifically.

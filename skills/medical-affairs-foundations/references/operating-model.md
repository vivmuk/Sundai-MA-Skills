# The Medical Affairs Operating Model

Read this when you need to know who a deliverable is for, what happens to it
next, or where it sits in the annual cycle. Getting this wrong produces work
that is technically correct and organisationally useless — a strategy document
written for an audience that does not make that decision, or a field brief
delivered after the interaction it was meant to support.

## 1. The functions

Structures vary, but the work divides fairly consistently.

**Field Medical (MSLs / MSL leadership).** Scientific exchange with the external
clinical and scientific community. Generates insights, supports investigator-
initiated research, responds to unsolicited requests, supports trial sites, and
delivers scientific education. Measured on quality of engagement, not on
prescribing. The largest source of primary intelligence the function has, and
consistently the most under-exploited.

**Medical Information.** Responds to unsolicited enquiries from HCPs, patients,
and payers. Maintains standard response documents, the FAQ library, and the
enquiry database. Often the first place a safety signal or an emerging clinical
question surfaces, because the questions people ask reveal what they are
struggling with.

**Medical Strategy / Therapeutic Area Medical Lead.** Owns the medical plan:
scientific priorities, evidence strategy, stakeholder strategy, and the
integrated medical narrative. Translates the evidence landscape and field
insight into what the function will actually do.

**Scientific Communications / Publications.** Owns the publication plan,
congress presence, scientific platform, and medical education. Governed by GPP
2022 and ICMJE.

**Evidence Generation / Medical Research (often with HEOR and RWE).** Company-
sponsored Phase IIIb/IV studies, real-world evidence, registries, and the
investigator-initiated study programme. Answers the questions the registration
trials did not.

**HEOR / Value & Access (sometimes Medical, sometimes not).** Economic
modelling, payer evidence, value dossiers, indirect treatment comparisons.

**Medical Excellence / Operations.** Insight systems, training, compliance,
metrics, and the tooling everyone else complains about.

**Medical Review (MLR / MRC participation).** The medical signatory function in
promotional and non-promotional material review.

## 2. Who consumes what

| Deliverable | Primary audience | What they do with it | What kills it |
|---|---|---|---|
| KOL engagement brief | MSL, before a specific meeting | Prepares 20 minutes before the interaction | Length. A brief that cannot be absorbed in the car park is not a brief. |
| Field insight report | Medical strategy, cross-functional leadership | Feeds planning and evidence decisions | Observations restated as insights; no strategic implication |
| Congress intelligence readout | TA leadership, sometimes cross-functional | Decides what changes as a result | Being a summary of what was presented rather than an analysis of what changed |
| Publication plan | Publication steering committee | Resource and sequence decisions | Not tracing to evidence gaps or audience needs |
| Medical plan | Medical leadership, annual planning | Sets objectives and budget | Activity lists that do not trace to a strategic priority |
| Evidence gap analysis | Evidence generation, medical strategy | Study prioritisation and funding | Proposing studies because data are missing rather than because a decision depends on it |
| Standard response document | Medical Information, then HCPs | Answers enquiries at scale | Answering more than was asked; unclear approval status |
| Advisory board materials | Advisors, pre-read | Enables genuine discussion | Being a presentation of conclusions rather than an invitation to challenge them |

## 3. The annual cycle

Medical Affairs runs on a rhythm. Timing determines whether a deliverable is
useful or merely interesting.

**Strategic planning (typically Q2–Q3 for the following year).** Medical plan
development, evidence strategy, budget. The window where an evidence gap
analysis or a landscape assessment can actually change something. The same
document delivered in Q1 changes nothing until the following cycle.

**Congress season.** TA-dependent and dominating. Abstract deadlines typically
run 4–6 months ahead of the congress; late-breakers a few weeks. Pre-congress
preparation, on-site intelligence, post-congress readout, and the follow-up
publication push each have their own window. Post-congress readouts have a
half-life measured in days — a brilliant analysis delivered three weeks late has
been overtaken by everyone's informal impressions, which are now fixed.

**Data readouts.** Topline, then full analysis, then congress presentation, then
publication. Each stage has different disclosure constraints, and material
non-public information rules apply well before public disclosure.

**Label changes and approvals.** Trigger a cascade: medical information updates,
field training, scientific platform revision, publication implications.

**Field cycle.** Quarterly or trimester planning, with insight review meetings
feeding the strategic cycle — when the plumbing works, which it frequently
does not.

## 4. Where deliverables actually go to die

Worth knowing, because avoiding these is most of the value an agent can add.

**The insight that goes nowhere.** MSLs record observations, the system
aggregates them into a dashboard nobody acts on, and field staff stop putting
effort into recording them because nothing ever happens. The break is almost
always the missing step from *observation* to *strategic implication with a named
owner*. See `insight-generation`.

**The medical plan that is an activity list.** Fifty activities, no strategic
logic, no way to tell whether dropping any of them would matter. The test:
for each activity, which priority does it serve, and what happens if it does not
occur? See `medical-strategy-plan`.

**The publication plan that is a list of papers.** Sequenced by data
availability rather than by the questions audiences need answered.

**The congress report nobody reads.** Forty slides summarising abstracts.
Leadership needed four paragraphs on what changed and what to do about it.

**The evidence gap analysis that generates a wish list.** Every gap is real and
none is prioritised, so nothing is funded and the exercise repeats next year.

## 5. Lifecycle stage changes everything

The same job means different things at different stages, and a deliverable that
ignores stage will be wrong in ways that are hard to articulate but obvious to
the reader.

**Pre-approval / pre-launch.** Disease-state education rather than product.
Severe constraints on what may be communicated about the investigational
product. Focus on unmet need, treatment landscape, diagnostic pathways, and
building genuine scientific relationships. Evidence generation planning for the
post-approval questions that registration trials will not answer.

**Launch.** Data dissemination, scientific platform activation, HCP education on
appropriate use, rapid response to emerging questions. Field insight is at its
most valuable and most time-sensitive — what clinicians misunderstand in the
first six months tends to persist.

**Established / growth.** Real-world evidence, sequencing and combination
questions, subpopulations, comparative effectiveness, guideline engagement,
long-term safety.

**Mature / loss of exclusivity.** Evidence maintenance, safety surveillance,
and increasingly narrow investment. Be realistic about what will be funded.

**A note on guidelines.** Inclusion in clinical practice guidelines is one of
the highest-leverage outcomes Medical Affairs influences, and it is
mediated entirely by published evidence and the scientific community's
assessment of it — not by engagement activity. Guideline committees work on
their own cycles, and the evidence must exist and be published well before the
review window. Any strategy that treats guideline inclusion as a near-term
deliverable has misunderstood the mechanism.
